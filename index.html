Australian Wine: Climate, Regions and Trade

This page follows Australian wine from the vineyard to the glass: where the grapes are grown, what is grown, what the grapes are worth, and where the wine ends up. Each year Australia crushes more than a million tonnes of grapes and makes over a billion litres of wine. More than half of it is sold overseas, to a list of customers that has changed dramatically in the past decade, from China's rapid rise and sudden fall to the growing importance of Southeast Asia.

Where the grapes are grown

Most of Australia's grapes come from three hot inland regions along the Murray River, while famous names like the Barossa and Margaret River are much smaller. Each circle's size shows the tonnes of grapes crushed in that region in 2026.

Those three inland regions, Riverland, Riverina and Murray Darling–Swan Hill, crushed about 72% of the grapes reported in 2026, but earned only about 36% of their value. Their grapes sold for around $330 a tonne on average, compared with about $1,480 a tonne everywhere else. They supply the volume for everyday, affordable wine, while smaller regions like the Barossa Valley ($1,680 a tonne) and Tasmania ($3,720 a tonne) are paid far more for less fruit.

Very small regions are drawn larger than their true size so they stay visible. Shaded regions without a circle (N/A in the tooltip) crush less than about 1,000 tonnes; Wine Australia doesn't publish figures for them.

Every state has its own signature

Each state has its own mix of grapes. South Australia, the biggest producer, leans on red: Shiraz (24%) and Cabernet Sauvignon (18%) make up over two-fifths of its crush. Victoria and New South Wales lean the other way, with Chardonnay their single biggest grape. Western Australia stands out for Cabernet Sauvignon and Sauvignon Blanc, and Tasmania, the coolest state, is almost half Pinot Noir, a grape that struggles in hot weather. Grown white, shipped red

In 2026, white grapes made up 53% of the crush, their highest share since 2012, because the smaller 2026 crush hit red grapes much harder than white. But the wine Australia sends overseas tells the opposite story: 55% of export volume in 2025–26 was red, and only 41% white. Rosé, sparkling and fortified wines are only a small slice of either. Where did the year's wine go?

Australia made 1,133 million litres of wine in 2024–25. More than half of it, 638 million litres, was sent overseas, and most of that was red. Australians drank 443 million litres, with white wine the favourite at home.

The 52 million litres left over were added to the country's growing wine stockpile, which reached 2.06 billion litres, almost two years' worth of sales.

The inner ring shows where the wine went; the outer ring shows what kind of wine it was.

"Left over" is wine made minus wine sold that year; some sales came from older stock.

Which countries buy it?

Around three in every five litres of Australian wine are sold overseas. China is the most valuable market again after its tariffs were lifted, while the UK buys the most by volume. Each line's thickness shows the value of wine exported to that market. Drag the slider to change the year, or use the menu to show the labels as dollars or as a share of all exports.

Value and volume tell different stories. In 2025 the UK bought the most Australian wine by volume, about 200 million litres, but paid under $2 a litre, because much of it is shipped in bulk and bottled in Britain. China bought only about a third as much, around 68 million litres, yet paid over $700 million for it, more than $10 a litre. Singapore and Thailand are smaller markets, but they pay premium prices and are among Australia's fastest-growing customers.

A changing list of customers

China rose to become Australia's most valuable wine market by 2016. When China placed tariffs of up to 218% on Australian wine in late 2020, sales there almost stopped, and China fell out of the top 10 until the tariffs were removed in 2024.
