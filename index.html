Where is wine grown?

Australia's wine regions and their climates.

How warm is each wine region?

Australian wine grapes grow across a wide range of climates. The warmest regions, South Burnett in Queensland (22.4 °C) and the inland Murray Darling and Riverina (almost 22 °C), have growing seasons around 8 °C warmer than Tasmania (14.4 °C).

Growing season temperature is the average temperature from October to April, while the vines are growing. It shapes which grapes suit a region: Pinot Noir does best at roughly 14–16 °C, Cabernet Sauvignon at 16.5–19.5 °C. Darker regions are warmer; hover over a region to see its temperature.

Averages for the 1997–2017 growing seasons. Tasmania is one wine region, shown as the average of its eight climate areas (12.7–16.2 °C).

Where the grapes are grown

Most of Australia's grapes come from three hot inland regions along the Murray River: Riverland, Riverina and Murray Darling–Swan Hill crushed about 72% of the grapes reported in 2026, while famous names like the Barossa and Margaret River are much smaller.

Each circle's size shows the tonnes of grapes crushed in that region in 2026.

Very small regions are drawn larger than their true size so they stay visible. Shaded regions without a circle (N/A in the tooltip) crush less than about 1,000 tonnes; Wine Australia doesn't publish figures for them.

Every state has its own signature

Each state has its own mix of grapes. South Australia, the biggest producer, leans on red: Shiraz (24%) and Cabernet Sauvignon (18%) make up over two-fifths of its crush. Victoria and New South Wales lean the other way, with Chardonnay their single biggest grape.

Western Australia stands out for Cabernet Sauvignon and Sauvignon Blanc, and Tasmania, the coolest state, is almost half Pinot Noir, a grape that struggles in hot weather. Each cell shows a grape variety's share of that state's 2026 crush.

Shows the 10 most-crushed varieties nationally; the rest of each state's crush is other varieties. Queensland is based on only 541 tonnes.

Grown white, shipped red

In 2026, white grapes made up 53% of the crush, their highest share since 2012, because the smaller 2026 crush hit red grapes much harder than white. The Shiraz crush alone fell by 35% (about 129,000 tonnes), while Chardonnay's barely changed, so Chardonnay overtook Shiraz as Australia's most-crushed grape.

But the wine Australia sends overseas tells the opposite story: 55% of export volume in 2025–26 was red, and only 41% white. Rosé, sparkling and fortified wines are only a small slice of either. Each square is 1%.

Sparkling, fortified and other wines are grouped because together they make up only 2% of exports.

Every grape has a home climate

Most of Australia's grapes, whatever the variety, come from the hot inland regions, so most of the big circles sit on the right. Pinot Noir stands out: it is the main grape of the two coolest regions, Tasmania and the Yarra Valley (about 7,300 tonnes there, ahead of Chardonnay's 4,400), and has the coolest average (19.0 °C). Bulk grapes such as Colombard and Muscat Gordo Blanco are almost all inland (21.3 °C).

Who gets paid the most for their grapes?

A tonne of grapes from Tasmania was worth about $3,720 in 2026, and from the Mornington Peninsula almost $4,170. The cheapest grapes come from the regions that grow the most, so the biggest regions earn far less than their size suggests.

Each bar is one wine region. Its height is the value of a tonne of its grapes and its width is how many tonnes it crushed, so the size of each block is the total value of its grapes. The dashed line is the national average price paid for grapes. Hover over a bar for the region's figures.

Widths are each region's share of all reported tonnes, so the smallest regions are only slivers. Regions crushing less than about 1,000 tonnes aren't published. Queensland's two regions are reported together.

Do cooler regions earn more?

Mostly, yes. Regions with cooler growing seasons tend to be paid more for their grapes: on the trend line, each extra degree goes with a price about 20% lower. But temperature is not the whole story. The Hunter and Swan District are warm yet earn well above the hot inland regions, while Coonawarra is cool but sells most of its large crush for under $1,000 a tonne.

Each circle is a wine region, placed by its growing season temperature and the value of a tonne of its grapes, and sized by tonnes crushed. The price axis is a log scale, so each step up doubles the price. Hover over a circle to find the region on the map, or over the map to find it on the chart.

Grey regions on the map have no published crush figures. Queensland is left out because its two regions, with very different temperatures, are reported together.

Where did the year's wine go?

Australia made 1,133 million litres of wine in 2024–25. More than half of it, 638 million litres, was sent overseas, and most of that was red. Australians drank 443 million litres, with white wine the favourite at home.

The inner ring shows where the wine went; the outer ring shows what kind of wine it was.

"Left over" is wine made minus wine sold that year; some sales came from older stock.

Which countries buy it?

Around three in every five litres of Australian wine are sold overseas. China is the most valuable market again after its tariffs were lifted, while the UK buys the most by volume. Each line's thickness shows the value of wine exported to that market, and the three biggest markets in each year are coloured.

Notes in the corner explain the key events in the years they happened. Drag the slider to change the year, or use the menu to show the labels as dollars or as a share of all exports.

Value and volume tell different stories: in 2025 the UK bought about 200 million litres but paid under $2 a litre, while China bought about a third as much and paid more than $10 a litre.

A changing list of customers

China rose to become Australia's most valuable wine market by 2016. When China put heavy duties on Australian wine in late 2020 (up to 218% from March 2021), sales there almost stopped, and China fell out of the top 10 until the tariffs were removed in 2024.

Methodology

How warm is each wine region?
Growing season temperature is the mean temperature from October to April, averaged over the 1997–2017 seasons. The atlas shows it as a bar chart without printed numbers, so each bar was measured from the image (accurate to about 0.05 °C; checked against 10 regions published as numbers). Bands are 2 °C wide. Sources 12, 13, 14.
Where the grapes are grown
Tonnes crushed per region as reported in the regional appendix. Sources 1, 2.
Every state has its own signature
Each cell is a variety's tonnes divided by the state's total crush. Source 1.
Grown white, shipped red
Grapes crushed are by reported tonnes (2026 vintage); exports are by volume (2025–26). Shares are rounded to whole squares so each grid adds up to 100. Sources 1, 3.
Every grape has a home climate
Each region's 2026 tonnes by variety joined to its growing season temperature (as in the map above). The Murray Darling–Swan Hill figures (reported as one region in each state) use the average of the two regions' temperatures. Sources 1, 12.
Who gets paid the most for their grapes?
Value per tonne is each region's estimated value of its 2026 crush divided by the tonnes crushed. Bar widths are each region's share of the tonnes crushed by all published regions, so each bar's area is its share of their total estimated value (the 72% and 36% in the annotation come from these figures). The national average line ($570) is Wine Australia's weighted average for purchased grapes. Source 1.
Do cooler regions earn more?
Value per tonne as in the chart above, joined to each region's growing season temperature. The two state parts of Murray Darling–Swan Hill are combined into one point. The trend line is an exponential fit (a straight line on the log scale); the "20% per degree" figure comes from its slope. Sources 1, 12.
Where did the year's wine go?
Production, sales and inventory figures for 2024–25. Source 4.
Which countries buy it?
Australia's reported exports by calendar year, converted from US dollars to Australian dollars at the RBA's average exchange rate for each year. Lines start from the centre of Australia and end at each country's centre (city for Singapore and Hong Kong). Sources 5, 6, 7. Yearly notes: dollar figures come from the map's own data; trade events from sources 8–11.
